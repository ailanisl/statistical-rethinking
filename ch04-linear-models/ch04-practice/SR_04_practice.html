  1. \(y_{i} ∼ {\sf Normal}(µ, σ)\)

  3. \(σ ∼Exponential(1)\)

4E2. In the model definition just above, how many parameters are in the posterior distribution?

4E3. Using the model definition above, write down the appropriate form of Bayes’ theorem that includes the proper likelihood and priors.

\[Pr(µ,\sigma|y)= \frac{Pr(y|µ,\sigma)(Pr(µ)Pr(\sigma)}{Pr(y)}\]

  • \(µ_{i} = \alpha + \beta x_{i}\)

4E5. In the model definition just above, how many parameters are in the posterior distribution?

  • 3 parameters: \(\alpha\) , \(\beta\), \(\sigma\)

4M1. For the model definition below, simulate observed y values from the prior (not the posterior)

\[y_{i} \sim {\sf Normal}(µ, \sigma)\]

\[µ \sim {\sf Normal}(0,10)\]

\[\sigma \sim {\sf Exponential}(1)\]

sim_y <- function(N) {
  sigma <- rexp(N,1)
  mu <- rnorm(N, 0,10)
  y <- rnorm(N, mu, sigma)
  return(y)
}

prior_y <- sim_y(1e4)
dens(prior_y)

\[\alpha \sim {\sf Normal}(0,10)\]

\[\beta \sim {\sf Uniform}(0,1)\]

4M4. A sample of students is measured for height each year for 3 years. After the third year, you want to fit a linear regression predicting height using year as a predictor. Write down the mathematical model definition for this regression, using any variable names and priors you choose. Be prepared to defend your choice of priors.

  • Outcome: height of student

  • Predictor: year (1, 2, or 3)

  • Assumed relationship: height INC linearly over time

\(h_{i} \sim {\sf Normal}(\mu, \sigma)\) the likelihood of the height will be normally distributed around the line
\(\mu = \alpha +\beta x_{i}\) Mean will be linear regression function of year (\(x_{i}\) is year 1, 2, or 3 for observation \(i\)).
\(\alpha \sim {\sf Normal}(158, 20)\)

What is the expected height when year = 1?

The intercept will be normally distributed around a mean of around 158 cm. This is just my guess, given the limited information provided: namely, the sample consists of students who can be children as young as 5 years to perhaps 25 years old. The standard deviation should cast a wide range of heights (a bulk of heights around 138-178cm) without being unreasonably lax.
\(\beta \sim {\sf Normal}(5, 2)\)

How many cm do students grow per year?

The slope should reflect that children, adolescents grow at a faster rate than adults, but also keep in mind that the sample may include young adults. I said around 5cm growth, +/-2 cm per year.

The slope should be positive. I could use a Log-Normal to strictly enforce positivity, but Normal(5,2) puts very little mass below zero so it’s reasonable.
\(\sigma \sim {\sf Exponential}(1)\)

How much do individual heights vary around the average line?

Sigma has to be positive, and shouldn’t vary wildly. Exponential(1) has a mean of 1 and a long tail, giving most mass to small values while allowing larger spread if the data demand it. Strictly enforces positive vals.
Code 
par(mfrow=c(2,2))
dens(rnorm(1e4, 158, 20),main = "alpha prior")
dens(rnorm(1e4, 5,2), main = "beta prior")
dens(rlnorm(1e4, 2,0.5), main = "updated beta prior: log-normal")
dens(rexp(1e4,1), main = "sigma prior")

4M5. Now suppose I remind you that every student got taller each year. Does this information lead you to change your choice of priors? How?

Yes, my beta would be Log-Normal(2,0.5)

A Log-Normal(2, 0.5) has a median of \(e^2≈ 7.4\) cm/year, which is realistic, and puts zero mass on negative growth by definition.

4M6. Now suppose I tell you that the variance among heights for students of the same age is never more than 64cm. How does this lead you to revise your priors?

I would change my sigma prior to be uniform: \({\sf Uniform}(0,8)\)

  • WarningDoes not change alpha prior

    The SD in \(\alpha \sim \text{Normal}(158, 20)\) is not about how much students vary from each other. It’s about my own uncertainty in where the mean height is.
